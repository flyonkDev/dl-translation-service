## Hero — 3 Variants

**Variant A — Scene-driven** *(SELECTED)*

The first roundabout outside Marrakech Menara Airport is where it happens for most UK drivers. You've adjusted to driving on the right — that part clicked within a kilometre of the car park. What hasn't clicked yet is that in Morocco, the vehicle entering the roundabout has priority over traffic already circulating on it. The opposite of everything taught in a UK driving test, the opposite of France, the opposite of Spain and Portugal. You're on the roundabout, someone is entering from your left, and they do not slow down. This is not aggression. It's the rule. Moroccan traffic law gives right of way to vehicles on the right at most junctions including roundabouts — which means the car nosing into your path from the slip road is legally correct. Your UK licence is fully valid in Morocco for English-language holders, confirmed by Moroccan traffic law and accepted at most major rental agencies. Knowing the roundabout rule before you sit down in the car costs nothing. Finding it out at the first junction outside the airport costs more.

**Variant B — Fines-driven**

Moroccan traffic police apply speed fines from 5 km/h over the posted limit. Not 10, not 15 — five. The fine starts at 400 MAD (~£32) and climbs quickly. What makes this significant isn't the amount — it's the collection method. Fines for foreign-registered vehicles in Morocco are issued and collected on the spot, in cash, at the roadside. There is no option to pay later, no postal notice to a UK address. You hand over the dirhams, you receive a receipt, the transaction is done before you've indicated to pull back onto the road. The police presence is real and active: checkpoints appear at the entrance to most cities, at intervals on national roads, and with particular frequency in the south. The IDP question for UK drivers is genuinely nuanced — English-language licences are legally accepted without one in Morocco, but rental insurance policies and smaller agencies increasingly require it for claim processing. The 5 km/h enforcement tolerance is not nuanced at all.

**Variant C — Contrarian**

Morocco gets described as challenging for UK drivers — the traffic, the cities, the different road culture. Some of that reputation is earned. Marrakech medina traffic is genuinely chaotic. Fez in the Ramadan evening rush is not a relaxing introduction to Moroccan driving. But the highways connecting Casablanca, Marrakech, Tangier and Agadir are modern, smooth, well-maintained, and genuinely pleasant to drive. The Atlas Mountain passes — Tizi n'Tichka south to Ouarzazate, Tizi n'Test west toward Taroudant — are among the most spectacular road surfaces in Africa with manageable traffic and clear visibility. The driving isn't the problem. The things that catch UK visitors aren't the chaos of the medina or the mountain roads — they're three specific rules most people don't read before they go: the roundabout priority is reversed, the speed tolerance is zero above 5 km/h, and the car should not be on the road after dark outside cities.

## The Three Rules That Catch UK Drivers in Morocco

**The roundabout rule**
In Morocco, vehicles entering a roundabout have priority over traffic already circulating on it. This is the explicit rule under Moroccan traffic law — Priorité à droite (priority to the right) applies at most unmarked junctions and roundabouts. It is the direct opposite of the UK rule (where circulating traffic has priority), the opposite of France, Spain, Portugal, Greece, and most of continental Europe where UK drivers typically drive. At roundabouts with traffic lights or specific signage, those signals override the general rule. At unmarked roundabouts — which include many outside city centres — assume entering traffic is coming through.

**The 5 km/h enforcement tolerance**
Moroccan traffic police enforce speed limits from 5 km/h over the posted limit. The fine for the first band of speeding (5–20 km/h over) starts at 400 MAD (~£32). This is collected on the spot, in cash, for foreign-registered vehicles. There is no postal option, no grace period, no payment portal. The receipt is issued at the roadside. Checkpoints at city entrances and on national roads are the primary enforcement mechanism — radar guns are used, and the officer can stop and fine in the same interaction. The practical implication: treat the posted speed limit as the actual limit, not as a number with a 10% margin.

**Night driving outside cities**
Moroccan road authorities and every serious travel guide say the same thing: do not drive regional roads in Morocco after dark. The reasons are specific. Many rural roads have no lighting. Vehicles — including trucks and agricultural machinery — routinely operate without rear lights or reflectors. Livestock including camels cross roads after dark in large numbers across the south and in the Atlas foothills. A camel at 100 km/h on an unlit road is not survivable. Within cities and on well-lit highway sections between major urban areas, night driving is manageable. On anything else, it isn't.

## Driving Rules in Morocco (2026)

**Driving side:** Right — opposite to the UK; right-hand drive UK cars need headlamp beam deflectors

**Urban speed limit:** 50–60 km/h in towns and cities; 40 km/h in some residential zones; signage varies — posted limits override general rules

**Secondary/rural road speed limit:** 80–100 km/h depending on posted signs

**Motorway (autoroute) speed limit:** 120 km/h

**Speed enforcement tolerance:** 5 km/h — fines issued from 5 km/h over the posted limit; no grace buffer

**Roundabout priority:** Entering traffic has right of way over circulating traffic (Priorité à droite) at unmarked roundabouts — the direct opposite of UK and most European rules; signposted roundabouts with yield markings follow those signs

**Alcohol limit:** 0.05% BAC for non-professional drivers; 0.02% for professional drivers; practically, Morocco is a Muslim-majority country and alcohol consumption, while legal in licensed venues, is culturally sensitive — police enforcement at checkpoints is active

**Phone use:** Handheld strictly prohibited; hands-free permitted; fines 150–400 MAD (~£12–32)

**Seatbelts:** Mandatory for all occupants; fine 400 MAD (~£32) per person not buckled

**Children:** Under 10 years prohibited from front seat; child seats required for younger children

**Stop signs:** Morocco uses Arabic-script stop signs — the octagonal red shape is the same as internationally but the text reads differently; stop completely regardless

**Tolls:** Moroccan motorway tolls (Autoroutes du Maroc) are paid at staffed cash booths — not electronic like Via Verde. Carry small change in dirhams; credit card accepted at some booths on major routes but not guaranteed

**Night driving:** Not recommended outside cities and major motorways — unlit roads, livestock, vehicles without lights

## On Police Checkpoints — What to Know

Police checkpoints in Morocco are common, legal, and generally professional. They serve document control functions — checking registration, insurance, and licences — and are routine for both local and foreign drivers. A few things worth knowing before your first stop:

**Legitimate stops:** The officer will ask for your licence, vehicle registration (carte grise from the rental company), and insurance document. You present, they check, you continue. This is normal and takes two to five minutes.

**Receipt for fines:** Any legitimate fine issued at a checkpoint must come with a written receipt (reçu/quittance). If a fine is being requested and no receipt is offered, ask for one explicitly — "Je voudrais un reçu, s'il vous plaît." If the officer cannot or will not provide a receipt, you can ask to pay at the nearest police station (commissariat). A legitimate officer will give you a receipt. An officer seeking an informal payment will typically wave you through rather than escalate.

**South and rural areas:** Checkpoints are more frequent south of Marrakech toward Ouarzazate and Agadir, and in the Eastern regions. Officers in these areas will register your passport details into their system — this is standard procedure and takes a few minutes.

**French works significantly better than English** in most rural checkpoint and rental desk interactions. A few words of French produce faster, more comfortable outcomes than English alone.

## Renting a Car in Morocco

**Hertz Morocco:** Available at Casablanca Mohammed V (CMN), Marrakech Menara (RAK), Agadir Al Massira (AGA), Tangier Ibn Battouta (TNG), and Fes Saïss (FEZ). Accepts UK photocard without IDP at airport locations. Minimum age 21; held licence at least one year. Credit card required for deposit.

**Avis Morocco:** Same photocard policy at airport locations. Minimum age 21. Credit card deposit mandatory.

**Sixt Morocco:** Available at major airports. UK photocard accepted. Some city branch locations list IDP as a requirement.

**Local agencies (Marrakech medina, Agadir, Fez):** Policies vary significantly. A meaningful proportion require IDP alongside national licence. Check booking conditions before departure — the desk conversation at pickup in Morocco is not the moment to discover a missing document.

**Practical tips for renting in Morocco:**
- Minimum age 21 at all major agencies; some require 23 and one year's licence holding
- Manual transmission is the norm; automatic available at major agencies but book in advance and expect higher cost
- Credit card for deposit mandatory at international agencies; holds of MAD 3,000–8,000 (~£240–640) are standard
- 4WD is strongly recommended for routes to the Sahara, Erg Chebbi (Merzouga), Erg Chigaga, and Toubkal National Park approaches; standard 2WD rental contracts frequently exclude piste driving — verify before attempting any unsealed track
- Fuel: petrol (essence) and diesel available at stations in all cities and on motorways; fuel stations are less frequent south of Ouarzazate and in the Draa Valley — keep the tank above half
- Tolls: staffed cash booths on Autoroutes du Maroc; carry dirhams in small denominations; card accepted at some but not all booths
- Motorway speeds: 120 km/h maximum; enforcement by radar is active on the A1 Casablanca–Marrakech and A3 Casablanca–Tangier corridors
- Night driving: avoid regional and rural roads after dark; city driving and motorway driving between major cities at night is manageable; everything else is not recommended

## FAQ

**Do I need an IDP to drive in Morocco with a UK licence?**
Not strictly. Moroccan traffic law accepts driving licences written in Latin alphabet — which includes UK licences — without requiring a formal IDP for tourist stays up to 90 days. In practice, rental insurance policies and smaller agencies increasingly require IDP documentation, and rural police checkpoints process interactions faster when a French and Arabic translation is available.

**How do roundabouts work in Morocco — is it really different?**
Yes, significantly. At unmarked roundabouts in Morocco, vehicles entering the roundabout have priority over traffic already circulating on it — Priorité à droite. This is the direct opposite of UK, French, Spanish, and most European roundabout rules. Roundabouts with specific yield or traffic light signage follow those signs. At unmarked roundabouts, assume entering traffic is coming through regardless of your position on the circle.

**What happens at a police checkpoint?**
You'll be asked to present your licence, registration document (from the rental agency), and insurance certificate. The officer may register your passport entry date in the south — this is standard. If a fine is issued, a receipt must accompany it. Always request a receipt. If no receipt is offered, ask to pay at the nearest commissariat. A legitimate fine always produces a receipt.

**Is IDP Companion the same as a government IDP?**
No. A government IDP is a formal document issued under the 1949 Geneva Convention by an authorised national body. IDP Companion is a private multilingual translation document that presents your licence details in Arabic, French, English, and other languages — used alongside your original licence. For Morocco, where English licences are accepted without a formal government IDP, IDP Companion covers the insurance and rental agency documentation gap directly.

**Can I drive into the Sahara in a standard rental car?**
Not without violating your rental agreement. Standard 2WD rental contracts from all major agencies explicitly exclude piste driving — unsealed tracks, sand, and the approach roads to Merzouga (Erg Chebbi) and M'Hamid (Erg Chigaga). A 4WD vehicle with specific piste coverage is required. Book this in advance; availability at Marrakech and Agadir agencies is limited, particularly in high season.

**Is Morocco safe to drive in during Ramadan?**
Yes, with some adjustments. Traffic in cities is light during the day and extremely busy in the hour before and after Iftar (the evening meal breaking the fast). Road behaviour becomes more erratic in the Iftar window — locals moving quickly to get home. Plan long drives outside this period. Petrol stations remain open but some roadside services may have limited hours.

**What language should I use at a Moroccan police checkpoint?**
French produces significantly faster and more comfortable interactions than English, particularly in rural areas south of Marrakech and east of Agadir. You don't need to be fluent — "Bonjour, voici mon permis de conduire et le document de traduction" covers the essential exchange. IDP Companion provides the French and Arabic licence translation that makes this interaction predictable.

**How do motorway tolls work in Morocco?**
Morocco's Autoroutes du Maroc tolls are paid at staffed cash booths. Unlike Portugal's Via Verde or France's electronic systems, Moroccan tolls are not fully automated — you stop, pay cash or card, collect a ticket. Tolls are cheap by European standards — the full Casablanca to Marrakech motorway run costs approximately MAD 85 (~£7). Carry small denominations of dirhams; not all booths accept card.
